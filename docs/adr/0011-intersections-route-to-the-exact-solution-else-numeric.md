# Intersections route to the exact solution, else numeric, and say which ran

`a.intersect(&b, tol)` (the `Intersect<B>` trait) works for every supported pair of curves and surfaces, in either order: concrete types, the `Curve3D`, `Curve2D`, `ElementaryCurve`, `Surface` and `ElementarySurface` enums, B-splines and trimmed curves. It solves the pair in closed form when the pair and its configuration have one, and runs the numeric search otherwise: the Bézier-hull search for curve–curve and curve–surface, marching for surface–surface. Every result is one of three family results (curve–curve, curve–surface, surface–surface, plus the 2D curve–curve one) and says which method ran (`IntersectionMethod::Exact` or `Numeric`) and whether every hit was found (`Completeness`, ADR 0007). An exact result is always complete, since a closed form proves that no other hit exists.

The typed per-pair results stay, under `IntersectExact<B>::intersect_exact`: a stack-only enum per pair, no parameter it does not need, never a numeric search. A caller who meets only elementary shapes in a hot loop keeps the nanosecond path one method away. The routing itself adds no run-time dispatch for concrete types: a pair of concrete types calls its typed solver directly, and a trimmed curve routes through its basis type, so only an enum operand pays for a `match`. The routed call is about 45–55 ns over the typed exact call alone in 3D, and about 17 ns in 2D, spent moving and building the larger result (docs/benchmarks.md).

ADR 0001 rejected an automatic numeric fallback because the switch was hidden: run time moved from nanoseconds to milliseconds and the curves from exact to fitted without the caller knowing. The switch is now visible on every result, so the objection no longer holds, and the name every caller reaches for first gives the whole answer. "Not analytic" survives only as an outcome of `intersect_exact`.

`intersect_curve_curve(a, b, tol)` and `intersect_curve_surface(curve, surface, tol)` stay public, generic over `ParametricCurve3D` and `ParametricSurface`, and always numeric. They are the entry point for curve and surface types defined outside the crate, and return the same family results as `intersect`, so there is one result vocabulary. Marching has no such entry point: it is crate-private, and `intersect` is the only way to march two surfaces.

A trimmed curve is intersected as its basis, then its range applies. A hit is kept when it lies within `tol.confusion` in space of the range: a hit beyond an end is measured as the parameter excess times the curve's speed there, and is reported at that end. This holds at every end, the seam of a circle or ellipse included, and agrees with the rule for overlaps clipped to a sliver: a clipped piece no longer than `tol.confusion` in space is a hit where the curves touch, not an overlap and not nothing. A parametric widening by `tol.parametric` (1e-9) is not a distance decision: the same parameter excess is a different distance on curves of different speeds. A hit 1e-8 past an end in space would be dropped on a circle of radius 1 (parameter excess 1e-8 > 1e-9) and kept on one of radius 1e3 (excess 1e-11), and a hit 1e-9 past an end in parameter would be kept on radius 1e3 although it lies 1e-6 away in space, ten times the confusion tolerance.

Marching, as the numeric surface–surface solver, no longer seeds from a parameter grid limited to `[-10, 10]` along unbounded directions, which missed every curve farther out. It seeds from the points where iso-curves of an elementary side meet the other surface, through exact curve–surface intersections only: rulings and circles of a cylinder or cone, circles of a sphere or torus (not against a torus, which has no closed form with a circle in general), lines of a plane across the other surface's bounding box. A line is intersected whole, so an unbounded direction is searched whole. A B-spline side adds its own seeds (patch pairs or the grid), and when both surfaces are bounded the bounded grid seeds are added too, because a pair such as sphere × torus loses curves with iso-curve seeds alone. A trace ends where it leaves the cube of side `Tolerance::MODEL_SIZE` centred on the origin, or where it closes; the step count is only a safety limit. The step follows the curvature with no fixed cap, is halved when the intersection at its midpoint strays from the chord by more than twice the chord deviation, and is halved when it crosses more than one knot of a B-spline side, unless the crossed knots span at most `tol.confusion` in space. Every numeric surface–surface result is `Sampled`: a loop smaller than the iso-curve spacing can be missed, and a grazing curve may not be traced.

## Considered options

- **Keeping `intersect` exact and adding a routed `intersect_any`**: rejected. The exact-only call returns no intersection ("not analytic") for a valid pair such as a conic against a torus or any B-spline, so every caller that needs the curves or hits must test for it and call a second function. Routing costs about 50 ns, small against solvers that take from nanoseconds to milliseconds, and the result's method keeps the switch visible; a caller who cannot afford it keeps `intersect_exact`.
- **A routed path only, with no typed path**: rejected. Every hot-loop call would pay for the family result, and the compiler would no longer rule out impossible outcomes such as a hyperbola from plane–sphere.
- **Routing every pair through the elementary enums**: rejected. It costs a `match` on both operands and a conversion for pairs whose types are known at compile time; the typed routing saves about 13–16 ns per circle pair.
- **Making the generic numeric functions crate-private**: rejected. A caller-defined curve or surface would have no way to be intersected; returning the family result keeps them cheap to keep.
- **Keeping marching public**: rejected. It would be a second surface–surface entry point with a different result shape and no exact path.
- **Iso-curve seeds alone on bounded pairs**: rejected. They lost curves of sphere × torus; the grid seeds add about 0.5 ms on that pair (1.02 ms against 0.52 ms), and correctness comes first.
- **A step cap tied to the distance from the seed**: rejected. The step grew past small B-spline features; the midpoint test and the knot-crossing rule bound the step by the geometry instead.
